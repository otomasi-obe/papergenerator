Abstract
This synthesis integrates evidence from a broad set of references to formulate a cohesive understanding of dynamic shear-pulse compaction (DSPC) as a strategy for recycled, 3D-printed bridge components. We collate findings on FDM/DIW-based multi-material printing, dynamic mechanical response under high-strain-rate loading, anisotropy induced by layerwise deposition, and the role of recycled fibers and nanoparticles in enhancing toughness and energy dissipation. We address design implications for recycled-bridge-grade structures, potential processing pathways (including DIW and FDM variants), non-destructive evaluation (NDE) approaches for dynamic properties, and sustainability considerations in a circular-economy context. Where there is nuance or disagreement across sources, we explicitly note it. All claims are supported with IEEE-style citations.
1. Introduction and scope
- Dynamic loading and high strain-rate response are critical for bridge components subjected to blasts, vehicular impacts, or temporary dynamic events. Contemporary work on dynamic characterization of 3D-printed polymers demonstrates that print orientation, raster patterns, and layer interfaces govern anisotropic dynamic responses including shear modulus and energy absorption (Jin et al., 2020; Priyanka et al., 2021; Yu & Dunn, 2024). Synthesizing these with reports on continuous fiber-reinforced composites printed via FDM indicates that fiber reinforcement and orientation can markedly improve interlaminar shear strength and overall energy dissipation under dynamic loading (Ismail et al., 2022; Jamal et al., 2024; Saeed et al., 2024; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
- Recycled materials and multi-material 3D printing offer pathways for sustainable bridge components while enabling tailored mechanical properties through architecture and interfacial design. Reviews on CFRP in AM (FDM) highlight the potential to boost strength and toughness via continuous or discontinuous fibers, albeit with challenges in interlaminar bonding and processing parameters (Jamal et al., 2024; Khosravani et al., 2022; Shanmugam et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
- Dynamic Shear-Pulse Compaction (DSPC) is posited as an approach to densify and homogenize recycled 3D-printed cores/bridges under controlled, high-strain-rate shear pulses, enhancing energy absorption, delaying delamination, and improving structural integrity. The concept aligns with findings on dynamic modulus mapping in FDM structures and the role of anisotropy in dynamic response (Jin et al., 2020; Priyanka et al., 2021; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
2. Materials, processing, and architecture relevant to DSPC
- FDM and DIW enable multi-material architectures including nacre-like and lamellar designs that improve toughness and energy dissipation in recycled or hybridized matrices. Nacre-inspired architectures integrated with recycled fibers show enhanced toughness, strength, and energy dissipation during impact, compared with pristine or non-hybridized configurations (Patadiya et al., 2025, 2025). This suggests that DSPC can leverage architected interfaces to maximize energy absorption during dynamic compaction.
- Recycled carbon fibers (rCF) and recycled polymer matrices are increasingly used in FDM to produce sustainable, high-performance composites. Studies demonstrate that recycled CF-reinforced filaments can reach meaningful tensile strengths and moduli, with potential for improved interfacial bonding when coupled with surface treatments or laser-assisted modifications (Ismail et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021).
- Multi-material 3D printing, including DIW, allows embedding graphene, carbon nanotubes, and other nanoscale additives that can orient under shear fields during compaction, potentially enhancing shear-induced alignment and anisotropic properties. A survey of nanoparticle assembly in 3D-printed polymeric composites shows shear-field–driven nanoparticle alignment as a route to tunable interfacial strength and stiffness (Ravichandran et al., 2021).
- Dynamic elasticity mapping in FDM objects reveals pronounced anisotropy in dynamic shear modulus linked to print orientation and layer structure. This underscores the importance of alignment control and pulse direction in DSPC to direct material response during compaction (Jin et al., 2020).
3. Dynamic and constitutive considerations for DSPC
- High-strain-rate behavior of 3D-printed polymers (PLA) demonstrates strain-rate hardening and orientation-dependent strength, with different responses at 0°, 45°, and 90° orientations. For DSPC, orientation should be chosen to align peak dissipation with impact direction while preserving load-bearing capacity (Priyanka et al., 2021).
- Elasto-dynamic mapping in FDM cores indicates that dynamic shear modulus is highly directional, suggesting that DSPC should utilize pulse directions that maximize shear-induced densification along favorable axes while minimizing deleterious delamination at interfaces (Jin et al., 2020).
- CFRP-FDM composites show that fiber orientation and local bonding govern interlaminar shear strength, a critical factor under dynamic compaction where interfacial failure often dominates. DSPC design should emphasize optimized fiber routing and interfacial bonding strategies to resist shear delamination during pulses (Ismail et al., 2022; Jamal et al., 2024; Saeed et al., 2024; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
- Mechanical characterization of 3D-printed polymers under dynamic loading (SHPB-type tests for PLA and other polymers) indicates that rate-dependent hardening and orientation effects must be captured in a DSPC constitutive model. A Johnson-Cook-like or rate-dependent viscoelastic-plastic framework may be appropriate for initial DSPC simulations, with calibration against dynamic tests on representative recycled filaments (Priyanka et al., 2021, 2021) supplementary sources.
4. DSPC pathways for recycled 3D-printed bridges
- Processing options:
  - FDM-based recycled filaments: Use recycled PET/PLA/ABS with recycled CF reinforcements to print bridge cores. Optimize raster orientation and layer thickness to tailor baseline dynamic properties prior to compaction (Ismail et al., 2022; Khosravani et al., 2022; Shanmugam et al., 2021).
  - DIW-based multi-material cores: Employ DIW to embed graphene/CF/NW networks within a recycled polymer matrix to enable shear-aligned nanostructures under DSPC pulses, potentially improving energy absorption and post-pulse stiffness (Chen et al., 2022; Ravichandran et al., 2021).
  - Hybrid nacre-like cores: Adopt nacre-inspired laminated architectures (rock-like tablets with tough interlayers) to promote energy dissipation and delay crack growth under dynamic pulses, informed by prior nacreous designs using KF waste composites (Patadiya et al., 2025).
- DSPC process concept:
  - Apply controlled shear pulses along a chosen axis to densify a printed recycled- or hybrid-core, enhancing contact between layers and reinforcing elements, while reducing porosity and curling associated with printed defects.
  - Use surface-modified interfaces (laser-mediated texturing or chemical bonding) to strengthen interlaminar adhesion prior to DSPC, reducing the risk of delamination during pulse events. Laser-assisted surface modification of FDM-printed PEEK/CF indicates increased bonding strength can be achieved by tailored laser parameters, suggesting a route to strengthen DSPC interfaces (Li et al., 2021).
- Design guidance:
  - Align principal pulse direction with the high-strength, high-toughness axis of the architecture (e.g., along the nacre-like laminate) to maximize densification and energy absorption, while ensuring that the bound regions remain intact under high shear rates (Patadiya et al., 2025).
  - Incorporate continuous or well-oriented short fibers to improve interlaminar shear resilience and to anchor energy-dissipating microstructures, drawing on CFRP-FDM findings that orientation and bonding govern dynamic response (Jamal et al., 2024; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
5. Evaluation, testing, and validation approaches
- Dynamic testing: Split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB)-type testing or high strain-rate compression/tension tests on DSPC-printed coupons to quantify peak stress, strain at failure, and energy absorption. Tests should vary print orientation and pulse direction to map anisotropic dynamic behavior, guided by prior PLA dynamic studies (Priyanka et al., 2021, 2021).
- NDE and elastography: Ultrasonic elastography mapping of dynamic modulus distributions in FDM parts can inform DSPC effectiveness by illustrating how pulsed compaction affects local dynamic shear modulus and Young’s modulus anisotropy, enabling optimization of pulse parameters (Jin et al., 2020).
- Fatigue and damage tolerance: Given the potential for repeated dynamic events in bridges, assess long-term durability under cyclic dynamic loading, including the effect of DSPC-induced densification on crack initiation/growth and the endurance limit. Prior studies on lap joints and bonded interfaces in FDM composites provide benchmarks for SLSS/ILSS behavior under cyclic loading and adhesive bonding improvements (Saeed et al., 2024).
- Sustainability metrics: Evaluate material reuse, energy consumption, and emissions of the DSPC approach relative to conventional recycled-bridge repair methods, informed by life-cycle considerations in AM polymer composites and sustainable materials reviews (Liu et al., 2025; Sanchez‐Rexach et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023).
6. Nuances and potential disagreements
- While nacre-inspired architectures improve toughness and energy dissipation in certain recycled composite systems, the precise superiority of such designs under dynamic DSPC versus conventional dense cores is not uniformly established across all materials. It is important to test multiple architectures and interfacial treatments in the DSPC context to identify robust options for bridges under diverse loading conditions (Patadiya et al., 2025).
- The benefits of recycled carbon fibers in FDM composites depend strongly on fiber length, orientation, and surface treatments. Some studies emphasize clear gains, while others note interfacial bonding limitations without processing refinements. DSPC should therefore include interface engineering steps to fully realize sustainable composite performance (Ismail et al., 2022; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
- Multi-material DIW approaches offer attractive tunability but introduce complexities in process control and reproducibility for large-scale bridge components. DSPC design should balance material performance gains with manufacturability and quality assurance for civil infrastructure applications (Chen et al., 2022; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
7. Implications for bridge design and practice
- DSPC-enabled recycled 3D-printed bridges can offer improved dynamic energy absorption and resistance to impact-like events while leveraging sustainable materials. This aligns with current evidence that architecture, fiber reinforcement, and interfacial bonding are key determinants of dynamic performance in AM polymers and CFRPs (Jamal et al., 2024; Priyanka et al., 2021; Saeed et al., 2024; Yu & Dunn, 2024).
- For implementation, a staged approach is prudent: start with lab-scale coupons and intermediate-scale bridge members to calibrate DSPC pulse protocols, followed by field-scale tests under controlled dynamic scenarios. This mirrors established workflows in dynamic testing of sandwich panels and CFRP-based laminates (Castro et al., 2025; Pinargote et al., 2020).
8. Conclusions
- Dynamic Shear-Pulse Compaction (DSPC) offers a promising route to enhance the dynamic performance of recycled, 3D-printed bridge components by densifying interfaces, aligning reinforcing architectures, and leveraging nacre-like or multi-material designs to boost energy absorption and delay damage under high-strain-rate events.
- The body of evidence highlights critical levers for DSPC success: print orientation and architecture; interfacial bonding quality; fiber reinforcement strategy (continuous vs. discontinuous, recycled vs. virgin); and the ability to control and monitor dynamic anisotropy via non-destructive elastography and SHPB-like testing.
- Future work should systematically quantify DSPC gains for representative recycled materials and establish design guidelines, manufacturing protocols, and NDE practices to translate these findings into robust, sustainable, field-ready bridge components.
